# Acukoto

Acukoto is a physically modelled koto (箏): thirteen silk strings, each raised on its own movable ivory ji, over a hollow paulownia body built as the instrument is made, and plucked with ivory tsume. The koto has been at the heart of Japanese music for centuries, and Acukoto tries to earn its name by modelling it from what has been measured of the instrument and its materials, and by fitting what has not to five professional koto recordings. It is DSP only for now: a JUCE-free C++17 engine with a demo renderer and analysis tools. There is no VST3, AU, CLAP or standalone build yet.

## Version history

### 0.19.0 — 2026-11-07 — The koto's techniques on the free keys

- **The right hand strokes as a koto player's does.** The thumb plucks toward the far side and comes to rest on the next string down, damping it for up to 0.2 s until it lifts for its next stroke (Kikuchi, Atlas Ensemble's koto guide); a string whose key or the pedal holds it is left ringing. In a chord the thumb takes the highest note and the fingers those below. Two neighbouring strings played together are one finger's stroke across both (kakite, warite): the nearer sounds once the tsume has crossed 16.5 mm to it, 8.7 ms later at velocity 0.7, 31 ms at 0.3. The left hand's presses follow a hand's minimum-jerk path (Flash and Hogan 1985) instead of an exponential glide.
- **The free keys A0 to C♯2 play seventeen techniques.** Latched (press again to let go): A0 sukui-zume, the tsume's back stroke; A♯0 tremolo with its corner, a stroke every 58 ms while the key is held; B0 pizzicato with a bare finger; C1 near the ryukaku (1.5 cm), C♯1 away from it (15 cm), D1 left of the ji, on the rest of the string; D♯1 the octave harmonic; E1 a finger on the ji; F1 staccato; F♯1 oshi-awase, the string below pressed to the note and both played. Gestures on the last string played: G1 tsuki-iro, G♯1 yuri while held, A1 oshi-hanashi. Across the strings: A♯1 a glissando far to near, B1 one near to far with the backs of the tsume, C2 chirashi-zume and C♯2 suri-zume on the strings held, or the last string played. Timings and shapes are measured on Pro Musica Nipponia's demonstrations for Miki's *Composing for Japanese Instruments*: yuri sways up to +75 cents and back to +15 every 0.55 s, tsuki-iro pushes 1.32 semitones in 35 ms and is back 50 ms later, oshi-hanashi lets a press go in 0.95 s, a muted note is 15-20 dB darker 0.1-0.3 s on, chirashi-zume is a 30 ms burst at 2.4-4.8 kHz and suri-zume 0.7 s at 0.9-2.5 kHz ([measurements](Docs/validation/technique-recordings-2026-11-07.md)). Places, the tsume's back and corner, the fingertip's distance from the ji and the scrapes' travel are estimates. The strings' notes, presses and the body hits are unchanged where no technique is latched, but the thumb rest and kakite change every phrase with neighbouring strings or a thumb stroke over a free string.

### 0.18.1 — 2026-11-05 — A quarter less CPU

- **Playing takes about a quarter less CPU, and loading about a tenth less; the sound is unchanged.** The body's modes now sum their drive and their pull on the strings in registers, and the ji junction's matrix products run as vectors: `process()` takes 25.6% fewer instructions on the Hankumoijoshi demo, and the demos' realtime factor fell from about 0.17-0.20 to 0.13-0.16 on the development machine. Designing both presets at load takes 11% fewer instructions (2.6-3.1 s became 2.1-2.6 s there). Every demo, song and body hit renders byte for byte as in 0.18.0, with GCC and Clang, and every design is bit-identical ([measurements](Docs/benchmarks/cpu-blocking-2026-11-05.md)).

### 0.18.0 — 2026-11-03 — Twelve hits on one octave

- **The octave under the lowest string is percussion; the keys below it are free.** The twelve keys D2 to C♯3 are twelve hits of a hand on the koto's body, four to each of three hands, climbing from deep to sharp: a palm (D2-F2), a knuckle (F♯2-A2) and a hard rap (A♯2-C♯3), each striking the top's far edge twice, the near side wall once and the back once, 22% to 76% of the way along the body from the head. The key's velocity is the hand's speed of arrival, so it is the hit's intensity. The owner chose all three hands by ear from the 0.17.0 set. A0 to C♯2 sound nothing now, kept for the techniques to come; the moved ji that 0.10.0 gave the keys below D3 is gone. The palm is 0.3 kg and the knuckle and rap are lighter on stiffer contacts (0.1 and 0.05 kg), so a contact lasts 9.6-12.5 ms, 3.8-4.9 ms and 1.8-2.8 ms at velocity 0.7. Each hit carries a touch on its arrival speed, taking 80% of the way to the strings' mean level as the strings' touches do: at velocity 0.7 the twelve stand within 3.6 dB (18.8 dB apart without the touches), at 0.3 8.1 dB and at 1.0 2.9 dB, and `AcukotoLevels --check` holds each within 0.5 dB of its intended level on both presets. The hands' masses, stiffnesses and restitution are estimates, no recording of a strike was compared, and the layout of hands and places on the keys has not yet been heard as a set ([research](Docs/research/body-strikes-2026-11-03.md), [measurements](Docs/validation/body-strikes-2026-11-03.md)). The strings' own notes, the presses, the keys from D3 up and the demos, which use no key below D3, are unchanged: the demos render byte for byte as in 0.16.0.

### 0.17.0 — 2026-11-03 — A hand on the body

- **The keys below the lowest string strike the body.** A0 to C♯3 played a moved ji, a string no koto has (0.10.0). Players of new koto music strike the body with the hand (Pornprasit, 2010, section 2.9), and each of those keys is now a place on it: ten stations from the head toward the tail on the top's far edge beside the first string (A0-F♯1), the near side wall (G1-E2) and the back (F2-C♯3). The key's velocity is the hand's speed of arrival. The hand is a mass pressing into the surface through a Hertzian spring, and its force goes into the body's modes where it lands, so the strings ring in sympathy as they do to any other motion of the body. What sounds is a low thud with a centre of energy of 93-145 Hz from the top and the back and 190-253 Hz from the near side, and falls 35-52 dB between 0.15 s and 1.55 s. At velocity 0.7 the keys stand from 11.1 dB under a plucked note to 7.8 dB over it (mean -27.9 dB, K-weighted, against -28.1 for four strings), the back loudest and the near side quietest; nothing evens them. The hand's mass, stiffness and restitution are estimates, and no recording of a strike was compared ([research](Docs/research/body-strikes-2026-11-03.md), [measurements](Docs/validation/body-strikes-2026-11-03.md)). The strings' own notes, the presses, the keys from D3 up and the demos, which use no key below D3, are unchanged: the demos render byte for byte as in 0.16.0.

### 0.16.0 — 2026-11-03 — An even scale, a lighter body

- **The scale is even.** Every string was plucked with the same force, but the pluck falls 4.5 cm from the ryukaku on all of them and each string's partials meet different body modes, so plucked alone at one velocity the loudest string of Koto Hirajoshi stood 6.9 dB over the quietest, and of Koto Hankumoijoshi 8.6 dB, its C5 5.2 dB over the scale's mean. Each string's touch, which the pluck's force already took, now takes its string 80% of the way, in dB, to its scale's mean level (K-weighted over the first 0.6 s at velocity 0.7; touches from 0.62 to 1.37). At that velocity the strings stand 1.4 and 1.7 dB apart; at velocity 0.3, 2.5 and 3.3 dB; at 1.0, 1.3 and 1.7. `AcukotoLevels` measures it and `Acukoto.Levels` keeps each string within 0.5 dB of its intended level. The 80% is the owner's choice after hearing a full correction and half of one; it is a level target, not a recorded koto's balance ([validation](Docs/validation/scale-evenness-2026-11-03.md), [decisions](Docs/decisions.md)). The demos come out 0.5 and 1.5 dB lower in RMS and 0.6 and 1.1 dB lower in peak, as they lean on the loud strings.
- **Lighter on the CPU.** The body's 356 modes now sum their forces over the 26 bar coordinates in single precision, four to a vector, and the strings' load is applied as the diagonal it is. `process()` runs 26% fewer instructions on the same workload, and a demo takes 34-38% of one core of the development machine where it took 51-53%. The sound moves by no more than one 24-bit step (-124 dB re signal) ([benchmark](Docs/benchmarks/cpu-bank-2026-11-03.md)).

### 0.15.2 — 2026-11-02 — Non-idle pressed plucks, dispersion bounds and faster setup

- **A pluck while the instrument is already sounding sets its loop delay, energy period and hand damping at once.** When `pluck()` ran on a non-idle engine (`controlCountdown > 0`), the newly plucked string kept its previous delay length for up to 15 samples until the next control tick, and its initial squared-velocity window (`voice.squares`) used the unpressed period (`pitch.roundTrip`), dropping the first `oldRoundTrip - releasedRoundTrip` samples of energy on a press or truncating the last `releasedRoundTrip - oldRoundTrip` samples on a `hiki-iro` pull. All four loops of the plucked string, its `roundTrip` energy window and the previous `handString`'s damping now update immediately in `pluck()`, and `noteOn()` resets `pressSmoothing` through `press()`.
- **Refined dispersion fits no longer stall at the zero first-order bound.** Projecting trial steps in `fitSections()` onto `p[0] <= 0` lets the four-pole Levenberg–Marquardt fit on B♭3 and E♭4 move past the boundary instead of rejecting the whole step: all six refined low strings now settle to `0.00–0.02` cents below 5 kHz with smooth negative first-order coefficients (`-0.42` to `-0.14`), where B♭3 and E♭4 had stalled at `a = 0.0000` (`0.08` and `0.52` cents).
- **Faster setup.** `designPlanes()` computes each stretch's constant- and bending-loss sections once for both planes, `setInstrument()` evaluates the `26 x 26` modal mobility matrix once per frequency for both planes using upper-triangle symmetry, and `Performer::prepare()` avoids calling `setInstrument()` a second time when `AcukotoEngine::prepare()` has already re-run it.

### 0.15.1 — 2026-11-01 — A press lands on its key's pitch

- **Pressed notes sound at their key's pitch.** A key a semitone or a whole tone above a string now presses that string to the key's own pitch in equal temperament, however the string is tuned: E♭4, tuned 11 cents flat as Ando's players tune it, is pressed 111 cents for E4. Before, a press was 3% past its interval from the string's own tuning, so pressed notes kept that tuning. Each of the fifteen pressed keys played alone on Koto Hirajoshi, dry and at Sympathy 0, now sounds within 3 cents of equal temperament, against 11 cents flat to 12 sharp in 0.15.0. The open strings keep their tuning. The owner's choice ([decisions](Docs/decisions.md)).

### 0.15.0 — 2026-11-01 — The first wave follows the hand

- **A pressed pluck starts at its pressed pitch.** The excitation now takes the press already on the string into account, including a held pitch wheel. Previously its first wave had the open string's period while its returns had the pressed period: a whole-tone press made the first wave overlap its return by 12.2% of a period. An isolated-string comparison with equivalent master tuning now matches sample for sample over the first 10 ms.
- **Hiki-iro on the downward wheel.** Pull down to relax the last plucked string by up to a semitone; return to centre to let it recover. Wheel-up keeps the whole-tone press. The hand contacts the stretch behind the ji in either direction and follows the next plucked string. This models the pitch gesture, without a friction/contact-force model or measured limits for each string. Two new, labeled Pro Musica Nipponia recordings demonstrate gentler 30–36-cent ornaments; the one-semitone range comes from their written technique guide, not a fit to those examples. See the [technique study](Docs/research/koto-techniques-2026-11-01.md).

- **Low notes remain safe under downward tuning at high rates.** The delay storage now fits A0 with the full master tune and hand pull at 192 kHz; the previous storage could overrun during excitation. This adds 7.44 MiB per two-tuning Performer. See the [validation](Docs/validation/hand-gestures-2026-11-01.md) for the regression, measurements and limits.

Existing downward wheel automation now sounds; centred and upward wheel mappings are unchanged. The body's materials, modes, tuning and fitted decay parameters are unchanged.

### 0.14.0 — 2026-11-01 — A re-plucked string rings on

- **The tsume stops only the stretch it touches.** A string still ringing when the tsume plucks it again now keeps its ring: the tsume meets it where it plucks, 4.5 cm from the ryukaku, and holds that stretch still before it slips off, and the rest of the string rings on into the new note. Before, the tsume stopped 70% of the whole wave, every partial alike, so repeated notes choked: over the 50-300 ms after a soft re-pluck of a loud A4, the sound fell 6.0 dB against the 200 ms before it, and now falls 2.9 dB; on D3, 2.4 dB, now 0.1. A bare finger still stops the whole string. The owner's choice of three candidates ([decisions](Docs/decisions.md)).

### 0.13.0 — 2026-10-31 — The box breathes as it bends

- **The arched top moves with the box's bending.** The top is arched along the body, so the box's bending strains it and moves the walls in and out. The box now changes its volume as it bends. Its first bending (94 Hz) and the air mode beside it (85 Hz) radiate, as Coaldrake found a koto's do, and each low pluck rings the body there. The shell's breathing moves from 293 to 299 Hz, off D4, whose fundamental now decays as recorded where it died 2.2 times as fast. Against the five recordings, the low strings' balance of fundamental and second partial comes closer: the mean error from D3 to B♭3 falls from 16.0 to 11.9 dB. D3's fundamental gains 5.7 dB on its second partial but dies faster, and G3's loses 7 dB to sit within 1.2 dB of the recordings' ([comparison](Docs/validation/low-notes-2026-10-31.md)).
- **The body's sound below its modes is corrected.** Each mode radiates through a complex weight found at its own frequency. Its part in quadrature was applied as though every frequency were the mode's own, which below the modes made a sound of its own. Against the full radiation sum, that was 6-9 dB too much at 50-63 Hz and 4-5 dB around D3's fundamental. The weight now holds at every frequency, within 1.6 dB of the full sum from 50 Hz to 5 kHz.

### 0.12.0 — 2026-10-31 — The left hand on the pitch wheel

- **The pitch wheel is the left hand.** Pushed up, it presses the last plucked string behind its ji, up to a whole tone at the top, on top of its note's own press, and lets it go as it comes back. Ato-oshi, oshi-hanashi and yuri are now played on purpose, in a melody over a held note too. Held up through the next pluck, the hand moves to that string and presses it before the pluck; the string it leaves lets go. ### 0.11.2 — 2026-10-31 — Presses reach a little sharp; a chord shares the strings

### 0.11.0 — 2026-10-31 — A pressed note holds its pitch

### 0.1.0 — 2026-10-29 — A koto, strung and built as one is made

## How it works

The engine is Aculyre's dual-polarisation stiff-string waveguide and modal body, rebuilt around the koto's construction (`Source/DSP/AcukotoEngine.cpp`, the instruments in `Source/DSP/AcukotoPresets.h`). Every value there is one of three things: a published measurement, a dimension typical of the instrument, or a fit, marked as such. None is a measurement of one particular koto.

- **Thirteen open strings, each on its own ji.** The strings are numbered from the far side, ichi (1) to jū (10), then to, i and kin. Each runs from the ryukaku, the fixed bridge at the player's end, over its ji to the unkaku at the far end. Each polarisation of a string is two waveguides: the speaking length from the ryukaku to the ji, and the rest from the ji to the unkaku. The ji stands on the top on its two feet: it heaves with the string's motion normal to the top and rocks with its motion across it. So every string drives the body under its own ji, and through the body every other string rings in sympathy.
- **Silk strings.** All thirteen share one gauge and about one tension, so the ji's place alone sets the pitch (Ando, J. Acoust. Soc. Jpn. (E) 10 (1989) 279). The gauge is 1.225 mm of silk, at a raw silk density of 1330 kg/m³ (1.567 g/m), held at about 216 N (22 kgf). D3 speaks over 1.26 m and A5 over 0.21 m.
  - A twisted silk bundle bends far more easily than a solid rod, as its filaments slide, so its bending modulus is fitted: 4.15 GPa, the median over the recordings' notes of the stiffness their inharmonicity implies.
  - No loss modulus is published for silk either. It is fitted to the decay of the recordings' fourth to eighth partials on the strings from B♭4 to A5, where bending loss is largest: 0.008 GPa, between what the four JpnMusic recordings (0.016) and all five (none) give over a recorded note's length. Nylon's loss would make those partials die two to three times faster than recorded.
  - Air damping follows Woodhouse and Lynch-Aird's fit to measured polymer and gut strings (Acta Acustica 105 (2019) 516).
- **Ivory ji.** A ji is 6 cm tall (Coaldrake) with its feet 5.1 cm apart. Ivory, which makes the best ji (Ando 1989), puts it at about 10 g with legs far stiffer than a plastic ji's. On its feet it heaves at 10.3 kHz and rocks at 7.8 kHz. Lower resonances are ruled out by the recordings: a ji rocking at 3.5 kHz would take the upper strings' partials from 2.5 to 4.5 kHz away hundreds of times faster than the recordings' 40-60 dB/s.
- **A paulownia body built as a koto is.** The wood is kiri with the properties Coaldrake measured for a finite-element model of a professional koto (JASA 148 (2020) 3153, Table I). The body is 1.83 m long.
  - The top is the arched outside of the log, with its grain along the length. The top and sides are one carved shell, and the back board is glued under them with its grain across. The ends are solid, and two holes are cut in the back.
  - The box bends at 94 and 231 Hz and twists at 184, 195 and 320 Hz, against Coaldrake's measured 100, 184-195 and 352-366 Hz.
  - The top is arched along the body, about 5.6 m in radius in the middle. A curved shell that moves out is stretched along, so the box's bending strains the top and moves the walls with it. The box changes its volume as it bends, and its first bending radiates, as Coaldrake's acoustic camera found (ICA 2019). The coupling comes from the top's strain energy, with paulownia's stiffness along the grain and the top's thickness. The top's own give along the body is left out, so this is the coupling's upper bound.
  - Those modes fix the walls' stiffness over their mass but not their thickness, so the thickness is fitted to the recordings' fundamentals. The fit gives a top of 15.4 mm, sides of 11.2 mm and a back of 5.6 mm, with the body weighing 5.2 kg.
  - The shell's section first breathes at 299 Hz. The air in the long cavity resonates through holes of 4.5 and 3.75 cm radius, at 85 and 146 Hz, against the 85 and 157 Hz Coaldrake identified; the first shares its motion with the bending.
  - Below 1 kHz, the walls' modes are damped at paulownia's own Q across the grain, 60, and the box's bending and twist likewise; Coaldrake measured 140 along the grain. What the joints and radiation add is not measured on a koto. Above 2 kHz, where the modes overlap, and for the air modes, the damping comes from the Q spread of Acustra's measured guitar bridge.
  - Above 3 kHz the walls' modes are too many to run one by one. Of those that mostly move the top, one in each run is kept and scaled to carry the run's motion, so the strings lose to it what they lose to the run, and it radiates unscaled, as the run's modes add in power. The runs lengthen in proportion to frequency, so the walls are finest from 3 to 6 kHz, where the upper strings' partials are strong.
- **Played with tsume.** The ivory picks on the thumb and two fingers have an estimated rounded edge of 2 mm, released over 0.03 ms. The player plucks just beside the ryukaku, the hand at one place whatever the string, so the tsume meets every string 4.5 cm from it, as a koto guide places it (3-5 cm, ["How to play the KOTO"](https://www.asahi-net.or.jp/~NP5Y-HRUC/kt-play1.html)): a share of 0.036 of D3's speaking length and 0.21 of A5's. The distance and the release are fitted together to the attacks of 724 notes in eight professional recordings, whose partials fall off faster with partial number the higher the string ([comparison](Docs/validation/attack-comparison-2026-10-30.md)). A harder stroke slips off faster and leaves brighter, as harpists' strings do (Chadefaux et al., JASA 131 (2012) 844). Each pluck strays from that place by an estimated 8 mm, the same on every string, and draws its own force and angle within the spreads Chadefaux measured on harpists. On a string still ringing, the tsume meets the string where it plucks and holds the stretch from there to the ryukaku still before it slips off; the rest of the string rings on into the new note. A bare finger holds the string longer and stops it all (Chadefaux; Laurson et al., CMJ 25(3) 2001). The player evens the scale by what is heard: each string's touch scales its pluck so that, plucked alone at one velocity, its level (K-weighted over the first 0.6 s, `AcukotoLevels`) lies 80% of the way, in dB, from where it would stand to its scale's mean; without the touches the loudest string stood 7-9 dB over the quietest, and with them it stands 1.4-1.7.
- **Oshide.** A note one or two semitones above a string's own note, which no string is tuned to, presses the nearest string below it behind its ji before the pluck, and in a chord the next one down when another key holds that string and the next will do. The press takes the string to the key's own pitch in equal temperament from wherever the string is tuned, the owner's choice: E♭4, tuned 11 cents flat, is pressed 111 cents for E4. It raises the string's tension and so its pitch, and it holds while the string sounds, after the key-up too, until the string has died away or is plucked again. With Legato on (`PlayingControls::legato`, off by default), the same note played legato, while the key of the ringing string is the only key down, is ato-oshi instead: the press moves over about 80 ms and the string is not plucked again, and the string's own note played legato lets the press go. The left hand also acts on its own (`PlayingControls::handPress`): the last plucked string, up to three semitones above its note's press or one below, following the hand within about 10 ms and resting on the string beyond the ji while it acts. A front end maps wheel-up to a two-semitone press and wheel-down to a one-semitone hiki-iro pull; the latter models the pitch gesture, not a measured pulling-force law. No hand holds a force still: the press's force fluctuates by 1.5% (young adults hold 1-2% in steady finger contractions, Galganski, Fuglevand and Enoka 1993), low-passed at an estimated 5 Hz, and the added tension by twice that. `RenderAcukotoPhrase` also plays a scripted yuri (a pulsing press).
- **Tuned by ear, as players tune.** Ando recorded six professional players of the Ikuta school tuning hirajoshi on one koto, three times each (Sec. 3.1, Figs. 7 and 8). Each string sits at their mean offset from equal temperament. The fourths and fifths are near pure, the octaves widen up the instrument, and the minor seconds sit near the Pythagorean 90 cents, so E♭4 is 11.3 cents flat. Each instrument then draws its own scatter about that from the players' spread, the same every time it is set up. Hankumoijoshi's two retuned strings follow the same method; Ando measured hirajoshi only, so they are estimated.
- **On the floor.** Played seated, the koto lies with its tail on the floor and its head raised on two small legs or a small pillow, about 8 cm over the tatami ("How to play the KOTO"; 1 cm at the tail, estimated). The floor reflects as a rigid plane: every radiating patch is heard again from its image under it.
- **Microphones and room.** An ORTF pair stands 1.2 m in front of the koto and 40° above it, with the head on the listener's left: two cardioids 17 cm apart with 110° between them. Each mode's radiation over the box's surface and from its holes is summed at each microphone as free-field monopoles, with each patch's own delay. The sum is found at the mode's own frequency and kept at every other, its part in quadrature through a 90° phase network. The pair is authored, then set beside a recording of Sakura (see Known gaps). A small room, authored rather than measured, is the space the demos are heard in. Width sets the stereo image, from mono to twice the pair's spread.
- **Playing.** A note plucks the string tuned to it, or one pressed up to it (oshide); any other key from the lowest string up to C8 plucks the nearest string below with its ji moved to the key, so every key from D3 up sounds at its own pitch. The twelve keys under the lowest string, D2 to C♯3, are a hand striking the body (below), and the keys further down play techniques (below). A note's key-up lifts the hand and the string rings on, as a koto's does, and CC64 holds every string. A palm laid across the strings (`PlayingControls::mute`) stops them. The Performer runs at 1, 2 or 4 times the host rate and ends in the portfolio's shared output stage (`Source/DSP/OutputStage.h`).
- **A hand on the body.** The twelve keys under the lowest string, D2 to C♯3, are hits of a hand on the body (Pornprasit, "Playing Techniques of New Pieces of Koto Music in Japanese Urban Culture", *Journal of Urban Culture Research* 1 (2010), section 2.9: "comparable to that of striking a large, open wooden box with a drumstick"). Each key is one of three hands at one place: a palm (D2-F2), a knuckle (F♯2-A2) and a hard rap (A♯2-C♯3), each on the top's far edge beside the first string twice, on the near side wall once and on the back once, at 22% to 76% of the body's length from the head (`Source/DSP/AcukotoStrikes.h`). A place is the mean of the body's modes' displacement under a palm-sized patch (five stations of 1.5 cm along the body by three cells across the top, the near side's height or four across the back). The hand is a mass closing on the surface through a Hertzian contact, `F = k d^1.5`, with Hunt and Crossley's damping at a restitution of 0.4; the compression closes at the hand's speed plus the surface's, so the body's recoil shortens the contact. The force enters the modes as a generalized force along their displacement at the place, and their velocity there comes back, so the strings ring in sympathy. The palm is 0.3 kg at 1.36e6 N/m^1.5, the knuckle 0.1 kg at 5.9e6 and the rap 0.05 kg at 2.4e7. Velocity sets the arrival speed, 0.02-0.5 m/s geometrically, times the hit's touch, which brings velocity 0.7 to within 3.6 dB of a plucked note's level on every key; a real slap is faster and far louder than a note, so the speeds are scaled to that. Mass, stiffness and restitution are estimates.

- **The right hand's strokes.** The thumb plucks from the near side toward the far one, the index and middle fingers from the far side toward the near (Pro Musica Nipponia, ["Koto"](https://www.promusica.or.jp/english/koto.html), technique 1), so in a chord the thumb takes the highest note. Its stroke over, the thumb's tsume rests on the next string down and damps it, "When a string is played with the thumb, the string directly below that string is struck with the thumb pick and is dampened" (Kikuchi), until its next stroke or for 0.2 s at most (estimated); a held string is left ringing. Neighbouring strings at one moment are one finger's stroke across both (kakite, warite), the nearer reached once the tsume has crossed to it at the stroke's speed, taken from harpists' release speeds of 0.2-5 m/s (Chadefaux et al. 2012). Every press moves along a hand's minimum-jerk path (Flash and Hogan, J. Neurosci. 5 (1985) 1688).
- **Techniques on the free keys.** A0 to C♯2, below the hits, play the techniques the koto guides list (Pro Musica Nipponia; Kikuchi), measured where Pro Musica Nipponia's demonstrations for Minoru Miki's *Composing for Japanese Instruments* (University of Rochester, 2008) show them. The latched ones change the next notes' stroke: the tsume's back (sukui-zume, a rounder contact, estimated 4 mm, slipping off over twice the time, the string moved the other way and no thumb rest), a tremolo with the tsume's corner (estimated 1 mm) back and forth every 58 ms, the median of a repeated note's strokes in Example 3.23, pizzicato with a fingertip's pad, the places near the ryukaku and away from it, the rest of the string left of the ji plucked 8 cm from it, the octave harmonic (the left hand's touch at the middle takes every odd partial: the fundamental 30 dB under the octave), a finger resting on the ji, staccato (the left hand stops the note at key-up) and oshi-awase. The finger on the ji is a dashpot of about 2 N s/m, a fingertip's damping (Hajian and Howe 1997), 5 mm from it, which takes the highs and leaves the fundamental: the fifth partial 30 dB down at 0.1-0.3 s and the fundamental 4.5, where Example 3.18's muted notes are 15-20 dB darker above 1.5 kHz. The gestures act on the last string played: tsuki-iro, yuri and oshi-hanashi with the shapes measured in Examples 3.13, 3.14 and 3.16. The glissandi stroke every string 58 ms apart (Example 3.30), far to near with the tsume's front, near to far with their backs (Kikuchi, "sweeping down across the strings with the backs of the picks"). The scrapes are the tsume's edge rubbing along the string: friction noise band-passed to the band each example shows, entering the string at a point that moves along it, so the string's own comb sweeps through it; chirashi-zume's ends as its edge leaves the string, as a pluck does. Their levels are fitted to the examples ([measurements](Docs/validation/technique-recordings-2026-11-07.md)).

## Known gaps

- **The tuning is one school's.** It is the Ikuta school players' tuning that Ando measured. In the five professional recordings, E♭4 and E♭5 sit 14-27 cents flat and pressed notes 11-18 cents sharp, where Acukoto presses to equal temperament. A recording of Sakura is tuned near equal temperament, within 6 cents.
- **The low fundamentals fade too soon.** D3 and G3 sit on the body's own resonances: the 146 Hz air mode and the 195 Hz twist Coaldrake measured. Each lends its fundamental to the body, so the fundamental falls fast at first. Measured from 80 ms over a recorded note's length, 0.9 s, D3's fundamental dies 5.4 times as fast as in the five recordings and G3's 3.0 times. Air damping alone, by Woodhouse and Lynch-Aird's measured law, takes 7-9 dB/s of the recordings' 10-12 dB/s, which leaves the body little room. A real koto does this too, on the strings that meet its modes, but these are the model's modes, not a measured koto's.
- **The upper strings' fundamentals fall fast at first.** From 40 to 150 ms after a pluck, the fundamental of the strings from G4 to D5 falls a median 3.1 dB and of those from E♭5 up 6.9 dB, where six professional recordings' fall -1.4 to 3.0 and -0.6 to 4.0 dB. It is the plane parallel to the top that loses it: plucked sideways, the string rocks its ji, whose feet 5.1 cm apart turn the sideways pull into a twisting push on the top, and E♭5's fundamental falls 19 dB in 300 ms; plucked straight down, it holds. The thin, strutless walls are the likeliest cause: at their full-weight thickness E♭5's fundamental falls 1.5 dB over that time, where it falls 11.5, but those walls lose the low fundamentals they are fitted for ([comparison](Docs/validation/attack-comparison-2026-10-30.md)).
- **The attacks follow the recordings' fall-off only roughly.** The attack's partials 1-8 fall 1.1, 6.9 and 9.2 dB per octave of partial number from G3 to E♭4, G4 to D5 and E♭5 to A5, against the recordings' 2.6, 6.0 and 8.0. No pluck distance from 4 to 5 cm or release from 0.02 to 0.05 ms fits all three registers more closely.
- **Single notes vary from the recordings.** Each partial lands on or between body modes, so one string's balance of fundamental against second partial can sit up to 15 dB either side of the recordings' median, D3 aside. A3, B♭3 and E♭5 are thin, and G3 and G5 full; A3 and B♭3 also sit 15-18 dB thinner than a gagaku koto's A3 and B3 in Reigakusha's recordings. Pressed keys do the same: A♭4 falls between two body modes at G4's ji and B5 where the modes near it move little air, so their fundamentals sit 17 and 11 dB under their neighbours', and B♭5's 15. Key to key, the balance varies about as much as between the pitches of a recorded koto, 8.8 dB RMS against 8.4 ([comparison](Docs/validation/low-notes-2026-10-31.md#pressed-keys-between-the-modes)). A real koto does this too, but at other notes.
- **D3, string 1, sounds thin.** Its fundamental sits 22 dB further under its second partial than in the three recorded D3 notes. String 1's ji stands 1.6 cm inside the side wall, where the shell hardly moves. Nothing in the modelled body radiates 147 Hz from there: twelve variants of the body, the radiation and the microphones lifted it 4 dB at most. The floor's own motion under the legs and the tail is not modelled and might. The target is thin, though: a gagaku koto's E3 in Reigakusha's recordings sits 34 dB under its second partial, further than the model's D3 ([comparison](Docs/validation/low-notes-2026-10-31.md)).
- **The silk's loss lies between two kinds of recording.** Against the four recordings that sound alike, the third to eighth partials die 1.1-1.3 times more slowly than recorded (median over the strings); against Omoigawa, faster. The fitted loss sits between them.
- **The silk's stiffness is one number.** One bending modulus makes the inharmonicity rise as the square of the pitch. The recordings' rises more slowly, so the strings from G3 to E4 are about 1.6 times less inharmonic than recorded, and those from E♭5 up about 2.4 times more.
- **The floor's air is not on the holes.** The floor a few centimetres under the holes would load them and lower the air modes; they stay fitted to the 85 and 157 Hz Coaldrake identified, and the floor is taken as rigid, not as tatami.
- **The body is lighter than a real koto.** It weighs 5.2 kg; a measured koto with its fittings, legs and struts weighs 7.5 kg. The four struts inside are left out, and the wall thickness is fitted, not measured. Coaldrake's published CT scan shows walls 1.5 to 2 times as thick, struts at 0.44, 0.80, 1.17 and 1.54 m and the holes near the ends. Put into this body, which has one section along its whole length, each of these moves its modes off Coaldrake's measured ones or takes D4's or E♭4's fundamental down 14-31 dB ([comparison](Docs/validation/scanned-body-2026-11-01.md)).
- **The keys above the strings are no koto's.** A moved ji keeps the rest of its string, from the ji to the unkaku, at the string's own length, and a pitch bend on a moved ji's key can sit a few cents off where it crosses a body mode. Above about C6 the strings are shorter than a koto allows.
- **After ato-oshi, other strings ring on at the old pitch.** It is not the pressed string: the strings in sympathy with it keep its old pitch once the press has moved it, at the same level whatever the press. On D4 it is the stretch beyond G5's ji, whose second partial is D4; on G4 and E♭5, the string an octave below; on A5, D4's third partial. With Sympathy at 100%, 0.4-0.8 s after a press, the old pitch stands level with the new one on D4, 4 dB over it on E♭5, 5 on G4 and 9 on G5, each pressed a semitone, and 16 on A5; on B♭4 and D3 it sits 4-12 dB under, and at Sympathy 0 it sits 19-48 dB under everywhere. Part of it is the pressed note fading: through the body, a string pressed by ato-oshi loses its energy 1.2-1.6 times as fast as it does open, where with its ji held rigid it would lose it 1.1-1.2 times as fast, so 1.6 s after a whole-tone press A5's string holds 19 dB less than an open A5's. A real koto rings in sympathy too, but a plucked E♭5 brings E♭4's string within 1 dB of its own energy in 1.2 s, which the light, strutless body (above) likely overstates.
- **The techniques are one player's measured shapes and estimated contacts.** Yuri, tsuki-iro, oshi-hanashi, the tremolo's and glissandi's rates and the scrapes' bands come from one player's demonstrations, on one G4, B4 or passage each, and every yuri sways alike. The tsume's back and corner, the places by and away from the ryukaku and left of the ji, the fingertip's distance from the ji and how far a scrape travels are estimates. A scrape's friction excites the string's transverse motion only; its longitudinal modes, which suri-zume's 1.1 kHz harmonic series in Example 3.27 suggests, are not modelled, and its levels take two examples as recorded at one gain. Uchi-zume, keshi-zume, a hiki-iro's friction and a strike's crush need a contact that moves with the string, and are not modelled. A keyswitch latched on one preset is not carried to the other when the instrument changes.
- **The hand on the body is estimated, and its layout unheard.** Each hand's mass, stiffness and restitution and the speeds are estimates, with no recording of a strike to compare, and the hand's tissue, a second strike overlapping the first and a hand that lingers to damp are left out. All three hands share a palm-sized contact patch, though a knuckle or a rap meets a smaller area. The owner chose the three hands by ear; which hand and place each key carries, and the touches that even them at velocity 0.7 (they stand 8.1 dB apart at 0.3), are the engine's layout and have not been heard as a set.
- **It is heavy on the CPU.** A demo takes 34-38% of one 2.8 GHz Xeon vCPU over runs (0.15.2 took 51-53% there), and its worst 256-sample block takes 1.0-1.9 times the block's 5.3 ms, as 0.15.2's did. These are development-machine figures, not a DAW's or Reason's.
- **Aculyre's sharping-lever state is still in the engine.** `StringVoice::pitch[1]` is never designed and no preset uses it.
- The demo levels are not yet calibrated to Reason's meter.
- There is no plug-in, editor, screenshot, main build or release yet.

## Build

```sh
cmake -S . -B build-dsp -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release -DBUILD_TESTING=ON -DACUKOTO_REQUIRE_PYTHON_TESTS=ON
cmake --build build-dsp --parallel 2
ctest --test-dir build-dsp -L '^(unit|audio|perf)$' --output-on-failure
./build-dsp/RenderAcukotoPresetDemos --jobs 2 Docs/audio/midi <out-dir>
./build-dsp/RenderAcukotoMidi <preset 0-1> <in.mid> <out.wav> [--dry] [--no-sympathy] [--legato] [--tail s] [--rate hz]
./build-dsp/RenderAcukotoMidi --report <preset 0-1>
./build-dsp/RenderAcukotoNotes <preset 0-1> <out-dir> [--velocity 0-1] [--seconds s]
./build-dsp/RenderAcukotoPhrase <preset 0-1> <script.txt> <out.wav>
python3 Tools/measure_koto_notes.py --dir <notes-dir> > model.json
python3 Tools/measure_koto_notes.py --compare model.json <recording.json> ...
```

CMake 3.22 and a C++20 compiler build the engine. `AcukotoDSPCxx17` compiles it as C++17 with `-Werror -fno-exceptions`, as the Rack toolchain does. `--report` prints each string's tension, inharmonicity, losses and dispersion design and the body's modes. `RenderAcukotoNotes` renders every string alone, for measuring against recorded notes. `RenderAcukotoPhrase` plays a phrase script by string number, with the left hand's presses. `Tools/measure_koto_notes.py` measures recorded and rendered notes alike and needs NumPy, SciPy and soundfile.
